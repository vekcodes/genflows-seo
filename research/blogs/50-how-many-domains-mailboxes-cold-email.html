TL;DR

  • Cold email is a capacity-planning problem. You scale by adding domains, not by piling more inboxes onto one domain. The math flows one way: target daily sends → mailboxes → domains.
  • Use ~20 sends per mailbox per day as your conservative working number (community estimates range 10–25 for Google Workspace, up to 30–50 cited for Microsoft 365) and 2–3 mailboxes per domain (directional).
  • 1,000 emails/day ≈ ~50–60 mailboxes across ~30 domains once you add a warmup/rotation buffer.
  • Never send from your primary domain. Register secondary sending domains, redirect them to your main site, and keep the primary out of the send path.
  • Warm new domains 2–4 weeks (treat 30 days as the floor), and stay under the 5,000/day-per-domain bulk-sender trigger — the multi-domain build does that structurally.

"How many domains and mailboxes do I need?" is the first real question of any cold email build — and the one most people get wrong. They either cram 20 inboxes onto one domain and torch its reputation, or they buy a single Google Workspace seat and wonder why 500 emails a day lands them in spam. The right answer is a short calculation, and this post walks you through it end to end: the math, the naming and warmup rules, the provider caps you must respect, and what it actually costs.

Sourcing note: the provider rules and thresholds here are VERIFIED against Google, Yahoo, and Microsoft's own announcements. The architecture ratios — sends per mailbox, mailboxes per domain, warmup timelines, and pricing — are practitioner and vendor guidance that varies by source; we flag those as (directional) and commit to conservative working numbers rather than presenting any single figure as settled.

The Short Answer

Divide your target daily send volume by a safe per-mailbox rate (~20/day), then divide the mailbox count by 2–3 mailboxes per domain, then add a 20–30% buffer for warmup and rotation. For 1,000 emails a day that lands around 50–60 mailboxes spread across roughly 30 domains; for 500 a day, about 25 mailboxes across ~16 domains. You send from secondary domains — never your primary company domain — redirect them to your main site, warm each new domain for two to four weeks, and keep each domain well under 5,000 sends a day so you sit below the Google/Yahoo bulk-sender threshold.

Why You Never Send From Your Primary Domain

Cold outreach carries reputation risk. One bad week — a spike in spam complaints, a blocklist listing — can start routing your invoices, contracts, and inbound sales replies to spam if that reputation is attached to your main company domain. So you isolate the risk: register standalone secondary sending domains that exist only to send cold email, and 301-redirect them to your main website so clicks and brand checks still resolve (directional). Many teams also route link tracking through a separate custom tracking domain so the primary never appears in the send path at all. The rule is simple: your money domain never touches cold traffic.

The Core Math

Everything downstream flows from three inputs. Here's the chain:

target daily sends ÷ safe sends-per-mailbox = mailboxes
mailboxes ÷ mailboxes-per-domain = domains
+ 20–30% buffer for warmup and rotation

Plug in conservative numbers — ~20 sends per mailbox per day and 2 mailboxes per domain (directional) — and the 1,000-emails-a-day build looks like this:

  • 1,000 ÷ 20 per inbox = 50 mailboxes
  • 50 ÷ 2 per domain = 25 domains
  • + ~25% buffer = ~30 domains, ~60 mailboxes

Don't chase the high end — reputation, not the platform cap, is what limits you. Start at 20/day per mailbox, watch your complaint and bounce rates, and only nudge upward if the data stays clean.

Domain Choices and Naming

Once you know how many domains you need, buy them right:

  • TLD: prefer .com; .co, .io, and .net are acceptable. Avoid cheap, spam-associated TLDs like .xyz and .info (directional).
  • Naming: use brand-adjacent variants of your real domain — getacme.com, tryacme.com, acmehq.com — so replies still feel legitimate and on-brand.
  • Age before sending: brand-new domains need warmup before any cold send. Google reportedly flags domains younger than 30 days, so treat 30 days as the practical floor before real volume (directional, single-source).
  • Redirect: 301 each secondary domain to your main site so a curious prospect who types it in doesn't hit a dead page.

Where the Mailboxes Come From: Workspace vs 365 vs Pre-Warmed

You have three provisioning paths, and most serious builds mix them:

  • Google Workspace (direct): best inbox placement into Gmail; Business Starter runs ~$7/user/month. Keep it to ~2 inboxes per domain.
  • Microsoft 365: stronger into Outlook and enterprise recipients, cheaper at scale, and some operators push higher per-inbox volume (directional). For a deeper comparison, see Google Workspace vs Microsoft 365 for cold email.
  • Resellers & pre-warmed providers (the 2026 trend): Google Workspace resellers run roughly $2.50–$4.50/inbox/month versus ~$6–7 direct, and pre-warmed Microsoft 365 inboxes are advertised as ready in 30–40 minutes with authentication auto-configured. Treat every "instant" and "pre-warmed" claim as (directional) and verify placement yourself before trusting a provider with real campaigns.

Warmup: Domain vs Mailbox

Two different things warm up, and both matter. Domain warmup builds the sending domain's reputation; mailbox warmup builds each individual account's sending history. Ramp both:

  • Timeline: 2–4 weeks for a brand-new domain before full-volume cold sending; established domains need less. Enterprise senders targeting thousands a day should plan 6–8 weeks (directional).
  • Ramp: start at 5–10 sends per inbox per day, increase by ~5/day, and cap campaign volume around 25–30 per inbox (directional).

Provider Caps and Compliance Thresholds

This is the VERIFIED backbone every build has to respect:

  • Google & Yahoo bulk-sender rules (effective Feb 1, 2024): senders of more than 5,000 messages/day to Gmail must have SPF, DKIM, and DMARC, one-click unsubscribe (the List-Unsubscribe header), and a spam-complaint rate below 0.3%. Enforcement escalated from temporary errors in February 2024 to outright rejections by April.
  • Complaint threshold: the hard limit is 0.3%; target 0.1% and never drift toward the ceiling.
  • Microsoft Outlook.com (effective May 5, 2025): domains sending more than 5,000/day to consumer mailboxes that fail authentication get hard-rejected with 550 5.7.515 Access denied. Microsoft switched from junk-foldering to outright rejection in late April 2025.
  • Google Workspace hard cap: 2,000 messages/day per user account (with a 2,000 external-recipient limit). This is far above your ~20/day cold-send target — proof that reputation, not the platform ceiling, is the real constraint.

Why the multi-domain architecture is a feature, not just overhead: spreading volume across ~30 domains keeps each one structurally below the 5,000/day bulk-sender trigger and isolates reputation risk. You get compliance and resilience from the same design. The authentication itself (SPF, DKIM, DMARC records per domain) is non-negotiable — see our step-by-step SPF, DKIM & DMARC setup guide.

What It Costs

Using the ~1,000-sends/day build (~30 domains, ~60 mailboxes), all figures (directional):

  • Domains: ~$10–15/domain/year → ~$300–450/yr (~$25–38/mo)
  • Mailboxes: 60 × ~$3 (reseller) ≈ ~$180/mo; at Google-direct $7 ≈ ~$420/mo
  • Sending platform / sequencer: ~$50–200/mo
  • Warmup tool: often bundled with reseller inboxes; otherwise ~$50–150/mo

All-in, a ~1,000-sends/day operation runs roughly $260–600/month, driven mostly by how you source inboxes. One warning: don't confuse "1,000 sends/day" with "1,000 inboxes" — you only need ~60 inboxes for 1,000 daily sends, and cost calculators quoting four-figure monthly totals are almost always pricing a 1,000-inbox operation. Frequently Asked Questions

How many cold email domains do I need?

Divide your daily send target by ~20 emails per mailbox, then by 2 mailboxes per domain, and add a 20–30% buffer. For 1,000 emails/day that's roughly 30 domains; for 500/day, about 16. You scale by adding domains, not by adding inboxes to existing ones.

How many mailboxes should I put on one domain?

Two to three — two for Google Workspace, two to three for self-managed Microsoft 365. Piling more inboxes onto a single domain concentrates reputation risk, which is the opposite of what the architecture is for.

How many cold emails can I send per mailbox per day in 2026?

Keep it around 20 per inbox per day. Community estimates range from 10–25 for Google Workspace up to 30–50 for Microsoft 365, but the platform cap (~2,000/day on Google Workspace) is far higher than what's safe — reputation, not the cap, is the real limit.

Should I send cold email from my main company domain?

No. Use secondary sending domains and redirect them to your main site. A reputation hit on your primary domain can push your invoices, contracts, and inbound sales replies into spam — a risk that is never worth taking.

How long do I warm up a new domain before sending?

Two to four weeks for a new domain, and treat 30 days as the safe floor since Google flags very young domains. Start at 5–10 sends/day and ramp by about 5/day up to 25–30. High-volume senders should plan 6–8 weeks.

What are the Google, Yahoo, and Microsoft bulk-sender rules I need to follow?

Google/Yahoo (since Feb 1, 2024) and Microsoft Outlook.com (since May 5, 2025) require SPF, DKIM, and DMARC for senders above 5,000/day, plus one-click unsubscribe and a spam-complaint rate under 0.3% (aim for 0.1%). Fail Microsoft's authentication check and you get a 550 5.7.515 rejection.
