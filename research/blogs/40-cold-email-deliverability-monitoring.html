Every deliverability guide ends the moment your first campaign sends. You authenticated the domain with SPF, DKIM, and DMARC, you ran the mailboxes through warmup, you built the infrastructure — and then what? Deliverability decays. Reputations drift, complaint rates creep, domains get listed, and providers change the rules underneath you. This is the missing operating manual: what to watch, on which dashboards, how often, and which signals still mean something in 2026 now that half the metrics you were taught to trust are broken.

Monitoring ≠ Setup: Why Deliverability Decays

The mailbox providers stopped talking about "reputation" as a soft, negotiable thing and started enforcing hard compliance thresholds. Google and Yahoo's February 2024 bulk-sender rules and Microsoft's May 2025 enforcement turned deliverability into a set of numeric limits that trigger automatic filtering or outright rejection. Monitoring in 2026 is therefore two jobs at once: staying under the enforced limits, and catching the slow drift — a creeping complaint rate, a newly listed IP, a domain quietly falling out of the inbox — before it becomes a cliff. You do that by triangulating a handful of leading and lagging indicators, because no single provider hands you a clean "you're fine" score anymore.

The Metric That's Dead, and the Ones That Still Mean Something

Start by throwing out the one number most senders still stare at. Open rate is no longer a deliverability signal. Apple's Mail Privacy Protection pre-fetches your tracking pixel on delivery whether or not a human ever opens the message, inflating open rates by a widely cited 15–35%. With Apple Mail representing over half of all email opens, your open-rate line is mostly machines. (Exact inflation range and Apple's share are Litmus-sourced and vary by audience — directional, but the direction is not in dispute.)

Google Postmaster Tools: Spam Rate, Compliance & the Reputation Question

Postmaster Tools is the free Google dashboard for any domain you send from — but there's a catch for cold email. Its spam-rate figure counts only authenticated mail sent to already-engaged recipients, so a low-volume cold domain often shows sparse or empty data. That's not a bug; it's why cold senders can't rely on Postmaster alone.

The two things to actually read there:

  • Spam rate — keep it under 0.1%; 0.3% is the hard line. Cross 0.3% and Google withholds deliverability mitigation until you hold under it again (commonly reported as ~seven consecutive days — directional).
  • Compliance Status — the newer, binary "do you meet the bulk-sender requirements or not?" view Google introduced alongside the 2024 rules.
The reputation-dashboard caveat. Google has been transitioning Postmaster Tools toward that compliance-focused view, and several third-party sources report the classic Domain Reputation and IP Reputation graphs (Bad/Low/Medium/High) were retired in late 2025. Google's own documentation still referenced them as of mid-2026, so treat those graphs as deprecated-in-spirit: glance at them if they're there, but build your monitoring on spam rate, compliance status, and external signals rather than betting on a reputation tier that may vanish. Re-check the live Postmaster interface for your account.

Monitoring Microsoft: SNDS and the 550 5.7.515 Wall

Microsoft is the provider that will bite you silently, because since May 5, 2025 it stopped junk-foldering non-compliant bulk mail to Outlook.com / Hotmail / Live and started rejecting it outright with:

550 5.7.515 Access denied, sending domain [yourdomain]
does not meet the required authentication level

If your SPF/DKIM/DMARC ever slips, Outlook mail doesn't quietly land in spam — it bounces, and you'll see it in your sending tool's bounce log. For IP-level diagnostics, Microsoft SNDS (Smart Network Data Services) is the free portal that reports trap hits and filter status for your sending IPs. SNDS is IP-based, so it matters most if you're on dedicated IPs rather than shared provider infrastructure — but it's the only real window into Outlook-side behavior.

Yahoo Sender Hub: CFL + Insights

Yahoo gives you two free monitoring hooks:

  • Complaint Feedback Loop (CFL) — Yahoo emails you an ARF report every time one of its users marks your mail as spam. It's DKIM-based, so you must be DKIM-signing to enroll. This is your direct line to who's complaining.
  • Sender Hub Insights (rolled out around October 2025) — surfaces your spam-complaint rate and inbox-delivered volume for DKIM-authenticated mail. Yahoo mirrors Google's guidance: aim under 0.1%, and exceeding 0.3% risks restrictions. (Exact Yahoo thresholds are secondary-sourced but consistent with Gmail's — directional.)

DMARC Reports: Free Monitoring You Already Enabled

If you set up DMARC properly you added a rua= tag pointing at a mailbox — which means receivers are already sending you daily aggregate XML reports showing SPF/DKIM pass rates and every source sending as your domain. Parse them with a tool like dmarcian, Postmark, or URIports and they become an early-warning system: a sudden drop in authentication pass rate, or an unfamiliar IP sending as you, shows up here before it shows up as a deliverability problem.

Blocklist Monitoring: Which Lists Matter in 2026

Getting listed is the fastest way to fall out of the inbox, and it happens without warning. What to actually watch — and what to ignore:

ListWhat it listsWeight
Spamhaus ZENCombined IP lists — SBL, CSS, XBL, PBL — in one query🔴 High — receivers act on it
Spamhaus DBLPoor-reputation domains (separate from ZEN, which is IPs)🔴 High
Barracuda (BRBL)IPs; widely consulted by receivers🟠 Medium
UCEPROTECT (L2/L3)Aggressive, high false-positive; lists whole IP ranges🟡 Low — often disregarded
SORBSDead — decommissioned June 5, 2024⚪ Ignore entirely

Quick tip on ZEN: it bundles SBL (known spam sources), CSS (auto-listed snowshoe/low-quality senders — the one cold email most often trips), XBL (compromised/exploited hosts), and PBL (IPs that shouldn't send mail directly, like residential ranges). The DBL is domain-based and separate. If you see a UCEPROTECT Level 2 or 3 listing, don't panic — it lists entire upstream ranges and most receivers weight it near zero. And if any old tool still checks SORBS, remove it: Proofpoint emptied all its zones in June 2024.

The practical way to monitor: run your sending IPs and domains through a multi-RBL checker like MXToolbox (or MailerCheck) on a schedule. If you get listed, use the specific list's delisting process — but fix the root cause first, or you'll be relisted in days. Root causes are almost always list quality and complaint rate, which ties straight back to how you build your list and verify it.

Inbox Placement & Seed Testing: The Ground Truth

Provider dashboards tell you about reputation and complaints; only a seed/placement test tells you where your mail actually lands. You send a campaign to a curated list of test inboxes across Gmail, Outlook, and Yahoo, and the tool reports the inbox-vs-spam-vs-missing split. Because cold email runs low volume across many domains, this is often your most reliable single read.

Build Your Deliverability Dashboard: The Weekly KPIs

Pull these into one view and check them on a cadence rather than reacting to a crisis:

  • Spam-complaint rate — target <0.1%, act immediately above 0.3%.
  • Bounce / delivery-error rate — keep well under ~2%; a spike means a list-quality problem.
  • Inbox placement rate — from a weekly seed test; the truest signal you have.
  • Reply rate — your real engagement proxy now that opens are meaningless; benchmark it against the outbound benchmarks.
  • Blocklist status — binary check on every sending IP and domain.
  • Authentication pass rate — from DMARC rua reports.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I check my Gmail sender reputation in 2026?

Use Google Postmaster Tools, but read the right things: your spam rate (target <0.1%, hard limit 0.3%) and Compliance Status. Google has been moving Postmaster toward that compliance view and the older Domain/IP Reputation graphs may be retired for your account, so don't depend on a reputation tier — triangulate with a seed/placement test and a blocklist check instead. Note that low-volume cold domains often show sparse Postmaster data because it only counts authenticated mail to engaged recipients.

What is a good spam complaint rate for cold email?

Under 0.1% is the target both Google and Yahoo recommend, and 0.3% is the hard limit above which deliverability degrades or mail gets rejected. In practical terms that's fewer than 1 complaint per 1,000 delivered messages as your goal, and no more than 3 per 1,000 as the ceiling.

Why did my emails to Outlook/Hotmail start getting rejected with error 550 5.7.515?

Since May 5, 2025, Microsoft rejects rather than junk-folders non-compliant bulk mail to Outlook.com/Hotmail/Live. The 550 5.7.515 error means your SPF, DKIM, and DMARC aren't meeting the required authentication level for that domain. Fix the failing record, confirm alignment, and use Microsoft SNDS to monitor your sending IPs going forward.

How do I know if my domain or IP is on a blocklist — and is SORBS still a thing?

Run your IPs and domains through a multi-RBL checker like MXToolbox or MailerCheck. Prioritize Spamhaus ZEN (IPs) and DBL (domains); weight Barracuda moderately and UCEPROTECT lightly. SORBS is gone — Proofpoint decommissioned it and emptied all its zones on June 5, 2024, so remove it from any tool that still checks it.

What's the best inbox placement testing tool, and how much does it cost?

GlockApps (~$59–$99+/mo), MailReach, InboxAlly (~$149/mo), and Warmup Inbox all run seed tests across Gmail/Outlook/Yahoo and report your inbox-vs-spam split. Pricing changes often, so verify before buying. For a free minimum, send a test to a real Gmail account and use "Show Original" to confirm SPF/DKIM/DMARC PASS and see which folder you landed in.

Can I trust open rate to measure deliverability?

No. Apple Mail Privacy Protection pre-fetches tracking pixels on delivery regardless of a real open, inflating open rates by an estimated 15–35%, and Apple Mail is over half of all opens. Use reply rate as your engagement proxy and inbox-placement tests as your true deliverability read; treat open rate as noise.
