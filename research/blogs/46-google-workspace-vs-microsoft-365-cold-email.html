Once you've decided to run cold outbound, the first infrastructure question is which mailbox provider to send from — Google Workspace or Microsoft 365. It's not a trivial choice: it drives your per-mailbox cost, your daily sending ceiling, how much setup friction you'll fight, and — most importantly — how often your emails actually land in the inbox. This guide compares the two head-to-head for cold outreach specifically (not internal company email), and gives you a clear rule for picking one, the other, or both.

Provider prices and the authentication rules below are verified against vendor and provider documentation. Inbox-placement percentages and the 60/40 mix ratio come from vendor tests, not independent audits, and are flagged directional. Safe daily send volumes are practitioner rules of thumb, not provider guarantees.

The Short Answer

If you want one provider and the fastest path to sending, use Google Workspace — it's the safe default, especially for lists that skew toward SMB/startup domains hosted on Gmail. Add or switch to Microsoft 365 (specifically Exchange Online Plan 1) when a meaningful share of your target list runs on corporate Outlook/Exchange, or when you want the cheapest per-mailbox cost. If you have the volume to justify managing two stacks, run both and route by recipient provider. Whatever you pick, the deliverability outcome is decided far more by your SPF/DKIM/DMARC setup, domain hygiene, and send volume than by the logo on the mailbox.

Deliverability: Each Provider Wins in Its Own Ecosystem

The single most useful thing to understand about provider choice is that spam filters trust their own kind. Gmail's filters are most comfortable with mail that originates from Google Workspace; Outlook/Exchange filters treat Microsoft-originated mail more favorably. Vendor placement tests bear this out directionally — one test reports Google Workspace landing around 89% inbox to Gmail recipients but only ~71% to Outlook, and Microsoft 365 landing around 84% to Outlook but ~76% to Gmail. (directional — vendor test, not independently audited) The exact numbers aren't the point; the pattern is: match the sending provider to where your prospects' inboxes live.

What is firmly established is the enforcement backdrop, and it's not optional:

  • Microsoft, since May 5, 2025: Outlook.com/Hotmail/Live began rejecting — not junking — mail from domains sending more than ~5,000/day that fail SPF, DKIM, and DMARC, returning error 550 5.7.515 at the SMTP envelope. Asking recipients to add you to Safe Senders does not bypass it. (verified — Microsoft Tech Community / Microsoft Defender for Office 365 documentation)
  • Google and Yahoo, since February 2024: bulk senders (~5,000+/day to consumer inboxes) must have SPF + DKIM, DMARC aligned at p=none or stronger, one-click unsubscribe honored within two days, and must keep spam complaints below 0.1% (and never above 0.3%). (verified)

In other words: neither provider will save you if your authentication is broken. Get the DNS records right first, then monitor placement continuously — that's what actually moves the inbox rate, regardless of which logo is on the mailbox.

Sending Limits: The Official Ceilings Don't Matter for Cold Email

Both providers publish generous-looking daily limits, and both are irrelevant to how you should actually send cold email.

  • Google Workspace allows roughly 2,000 external recipients/day (3,000 total unique recipients including internal), counting every To/CC/BCC address individually. New accounts are throttled harder until the domain has cumulatively paid a threshold amount. (reported; consistent with Google's published Workspace limits)
  • Microsoft 365 / Exchange Online allows 10,000 recipients/day, 500 recipients per message, and 30 messages/minute — but there's a critical catch for outbound: "non-relationship" recipients (anyone who has never emailed you — i.e., every cold prospect) are capped at roughly 1,000/day regardless of the 10,000 ceiling. (verified — Microsoft Learn)
The number that actually governs your volume: safe cold-send volume is about 20–30 emails per mailbox per day after a 3–4 week warm-up — an order of magnitude below either provider's ceiling. You scale volume by adding mailboxes and domains, never by pushing a single inbox harder. A provider's daily cap only matters the day you've already made a deliverability mistake. (directional practitioner guidance)

Setup & Infrastructure: The Rules Are the Same, the Friction Isn't

The infrastructure principles are provider-agnostic and non-negotiable:

  1. Never send from your primary domain. A spam flag on your sending domain would poison your corporate, transactional, and marketing mail. Use dedicated secondary "burner" domains that resemble the brand (e.g., get-yourbrand.com).
  2. Keep the fleet small per domain. The standard layout is roughly 3–5 (up to 8) sending domains with 2–3 mailboxes each, every one with correctly aligned SPF, DKIM, and DMARC and a realistic human-looking mailbox name. (directional)
  3. Warm up every mailbox for 3–4 weeks before real sends, and run a sender-rotation platform across the fleet.

Where the two providers differ is friction. Google Workspace configures DKIM/SPF/DMARC largely from a single Admin Console with clear guidance, so it's the more approachable setup. Microsoft 365 spreads the equivalent configuration across the Microsoft 365 admin center, the Exchange admin center, and Defender, with more manual DNS work — so provisioning a large Microsoft fleet takes longer. (directional) This is a big part of why agencies default to Google Workspace as the primary base. For the deeper build, see our cold email infrastructure guide and warm-up tools comparison.

The Managed & Pre-Warmed Mailbox Market

After Google/Yahoo (2024) and Microsoft (2025) tightened the rules, a managed-infrastructure market matured to remove the provisioning pain. The landscape, all directional and vendor-described:

  • Managed Google Workspace / Microsoft 365 mailbox providers that sell pre-warmed, ready-to-send mailboxes on real Google/Microsoft tenants. The appeal is speed plus the ecosystem-matching benefit above.
  • Private / dedicated email infrastructure and dedicated IPs — provider-managed or self-hosted SMTP with owned IP reputation, for teams that want more control.
  • Amazon SES and dedicated-IP relays — technically capable but built for transactional mail, not cold outbound. SES gives you no domain fleet, mailbox provisioning, warm-up, rotation, or list hygiene, and little protection when a list looks risky. Generally not recommended as a standalone cold-email stack. (directional)

Which Should You Choose?

  • Want one provider and the fastest start → Google Workspace. It's the safe default, especially for SMB/startup/Gmail-hosted target lists.
  • Targeting enterprise/corporate accounts on Outlook/Exchange → Microsoft 365, sending from the same ecosystem your prospects use.
  • Cost-sensitive at scale → Microsoft Exchange Online Plan 1 ($4) is the cheapest compliant mailbox.
  • Mixed list and enough volume to manage two stacks → run both and route by recipient provider (a ~60/40 Google-to-Microsoft split is a common starting mix). It also hedges you against a single provider's policy change taking down your whole operation. (directional)

Provider choice is one decision inside a much bigger system. Once your mailboxes are live, the compounding work is building a clean, verified list, staying compliant, and picking the sending platform that rotates across your fleet.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Google Workspace or Microsoft 365 better for cold email?

Directionally, Google Workspace is the better default — easier setup, broad tool support, and slightly better aggregate inbox placement — which is why most agencies start there. But it isn't universal: Google Workspace lands best into Gmail recipients, while Microsoft 365 lands best into Outlook/Exchange recipients. For mixed prospect lists, the strongest answer is to run both and match the sending provider to where each recipient's inbox lives.

Can I send cold email from Microsoft 365 without getting banned?

Yes, if you do it correctly: send from secondary domains (never your primary), fully configure SPF, DKIM, and DMARC, warm up each mailbox for 3–4 weeks, and stay well under the limits — especially the roughly 1,000/day cap on cold "non-relationship" recipients and the 30-messages-per-minute throttle. Since May 2025, Microsoft hard-rejects non-compliant bulk mail with error 550 5.7.515, so authentication is mandatory.

How many mailboxes per domain should I run for cold email?

Two to three mailboxes per sending domain is the standard, spread across roughly 3–5 (up to 8) dedicated secondary domains. You scale sending volume by adding domains and mailboxes, not by increasing the volume from any single inbox.

How many cold emails can I safely send per mailbox per day?

About 20–30 per mailbox per day after a 3–4 week warm-up — far below either provider's official ceiling (Google Workspace allows roughly 2,000 external recipients/day; Microsoft caps cold recipients near 1,000/day). Real volume comes from running more mailboxes, not from pushing one inbox harder.

What's the cheapest mailbox for cold email?

Microsoft Exchange Online Plan 1 at $4/user/month is the cheapest compliant sending mailbox, undercutting Google Workspace Business Starter at $7. It still requires full SPF/DKIM/DMARC and dedicated secondary domains — cheap mailboxes don't excuse skipping authentication.

Do I need SPF, DKIM, and DMARC for both providers?

Yes, on both, with no exceptions. Google and Yahoo (February 2024) require SPF plus DKIM with DMARC aligned at p=none minimum, one-click unsubscribe, and a complaint rate under 0.3%. Microsoft (May 2025) rejects non-compliant bulk mail outright. All three records are mandatory for cold email to reach the inbox on either platform.
